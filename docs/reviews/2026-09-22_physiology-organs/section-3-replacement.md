## 3. The kidneys in glucose homeostasis

The kidneys affect systemic glucose through three distinct processes: production, metabolic uptake and urinary excretion. Filtration and tubular reabsorption are much larger recycling fluxes, but ordinarily have little net effect on systemic glucose because nearly all filtered glucose returns to the circulation. Distinguishing these processes is essential in T1D: renal glucose production can support circulating glucose during fasting, whereas glucosuria lowers glucose while contributing to water loss. Neither process replaces insulin's suppression of lipolysis and ketogenesis.

### Filtration, transport and the onset of glucosuria

The filtered glucose load is the product of glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and plasma glucose concentration. At 180 L/day and 5.5 mmol/L, the calculated load is 990 mmol/day, or approximately 178 g/day using a glucose molecular mass of 180.16 g/mol. The commonly cited 180 g/day is therefore a conditional physiological example, not an invariant renal flux ([Gerich, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20546255/)).

Glucose enters proximal tubular cells through apical sodium–glucose cotransporters, using the sodium electrochemical gradient maintained by basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. SGLT2 (*SLC5A2*) in the early proximal tubule handles most of the normal filtered load; SGLT1 (*SLC5A1*) in the later proximal tubule recovers glucose remaining downstream. GLUT2 (*SLC2A2*) provides a basolateral route to interstitial fluid and peritubular blood. The serial arrangement matters more than assigning a universal 90:10 division: the amount reaching SGLT1 changes with filtered load and SGLT2 inhibition, and its reserve transport capacity is substantial ([Ghezzi et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30132032/), sections on renal glucose transport and pharmacology).

Transporter affinity must be distinguished from whole-kidney capacity. Ghezzi et al. summarize measurements of expressed human transporters under specified experimental conditions—150 mmol/L extracellular sodium, −50 mV membrane potential and 37°C—with glucose half-saturation concentrations of approximately 5 mmol/L for SGLT2 and 2 mmol/L for SGLT1. Their sodium:glucose coupling ratios are 1:1 and 2:1, respectively. These cell-level kinetic values are not plasma thresholds for glucosuria; different assay conditions help explain differing affinity estimates in older reviews. Nor does a small normal SGLT1 contribution imply a low maximum capacity when more glucose is delivered to the late tubule.

The whole-kidney maximum glucose reabsorptive rate, TmG, is an aggregate transport capacity. Urinary glucose begins to appear *before* that maximum is reached because individual nephrons differ in filtration and transport capacity. The resulting curved transition between near-complete recovery and saturated reabsorption is termed **splay**. Reviews often use approximately 10–11 mmol/L as an illustrative onset range and approximately 375 mg/min as an absolute adult TmG; neither is a universal value or interchangeable with a body-surface-area-indexed rate ([Gerich, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20546255/); [Poudel, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23961473/)).

Direct T1D observations illustrate the variation. Johansen et al. infused glucose gradually in 23 people with T1D and detected the threshold at the first trace of urinary glucose. Thresholds ranged from 6.0 to 14.3 mmol/L, and maximal reabsorption from 0.93 to 1.98 mmol/min ([Johansen et al., 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6714538/), original abstract inspected; full-text methods appraisal pending). These are observed ranges in one small study, not diagnostic limits. Urinary glucose alone cannot identify contemporaneous plasma glucose without knowing the individual's filtration and excretion relationship.

### Renal production and consumption

The renal cortex produces glucose through gluconeogenesis, using substrates including lactate, glutamine and glycerol. Insulin suppresses renal glucose production, while epinephrine can stimulate it. Renal glucose uptake, particularly in glycolytic medullary tissue, occurs simultaneously. Consequently, net renal glucose release can be small even when gross renal production is substantial; arteriovenous balance alone does not resolve both opposing fluxes ([Gerich, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20546255/), renal gluconeogenesis section and Table 2).

In the overnight-fasted human physiology summarized by Gerich, renal production is approximately 2.0–2.5 μmol/kg/min, representing 20–25% of total endogenous glucose production; the remainder is hepatic. This is a fasting-state estimate rather than a fixed T1D parameter, and renal contribution increases during prolonged fasting. Reports of substantially increased renal production and uptake in T2D establish that both fluxes can change together. They do not directly quantify either flux in insulin-deficient T1D. Reduced insulin suppression provides a mechanistic reason to expect altered renal production in T1D, but its magnitude requires T1D-specific measurements.

### Hyperfiltration and renal disease in T1D

Hyperfiltration describes abnormally high filtration, but its classification depends on the measurement method, threshold, age, body-size indexing and disease stage. Tonneijck et al. report prevalences of 10–67% across T1D studies; this wide range should not be interpreted as the probability for a contemporary individual. Their synthesis distinguishes three interacting mechanisms ([Tonneijck et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28143897/), Table 1 and pathogenesis sections):

1. Increased proximal sodium–glucose reabsorption reduces sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa. Reduced tubuloglomerular feedback can lower afferent arteriolar resistance and increase filtration.
2. Vasoactive pathways, including nitric oxide, prostaglandins and the renin–angiotensin system, alter afferent and efferent resistance and glomerular capillary pressure.
3. Tubular and glomerular growth alters transport capacity and filtration surface. Evidence that hypertrophy precedes hyperfiltration comes principally from animal experiments; temporal precedence is harder to establish in humans.

Glycaemic improvement can reduce hyperfiltration. The review cites an insulin-pump study in which HbA1c fell from approximately 10% to 7% and measured GFR from 149 to 129 mL/min/1.73 m²; the latter corresponds to a calculated 13.4% reduction, not 16%. The original experiment was not independently inspected here, so these paired values remain a review-reported example rather than a calibrated treatment effect.

Whether hyperfiltration independently predicts progressive kidney disease is less certain than its mechanistic plausibility. Some measured-GFR cohorts associate it with later albuminuria, whereas larger estimated-GFR studies have not consistently reproduced that association. Confounding by glycaemia, disease stage and the method used to estimate filtration complicates causal interpretation. Hyperfiltration, albuminuria and subsequent GFR decline are related but distinct endpoints; an acute haemodynamic reduction in GFR is not itself evidence of long-term renal protection ([Tonneijck et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28143897/), clinical significance section).

Increased reabsorptive capacity has also been reported in diabetes. However, the expression evidence summarized by Poudel includes cultured urinary proximal tubular cells from people with **T2D**, and should not be described as a uniform increase in SGLT2 mRNA and protein in intact T1D kidneys. Historical increases in TmG cannot by themselves identify which transporter, cell number or regulatory process accounts for the difference ([Poudel, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23961473/), “Renal glucose handling in diabetes”).

### Glucosuria, water balance and SGLT inhibition

Glucose retained in tubular fluid contributes to osmotic diuresis, but there is no fixed conversion from grams of glucose excreted to millilitres of water lost. Urine volume depends on the combined excretion of solutes and achievable urine concentration; net dehydration additionally depends on fluid intake and extrarenal losses. A factor such as 15 mL/g would silently impose a particular urinary glucose concentration and would not specify electrolyte-free water clearance. It cannot predict daily fluid loss from plasma glucose alone. Severe hyperglycaemia can therefore produce polyuria and dehydration without supporting a universal 200–500 g/day glucose or 3–7 L/day water-loss range at 25 mmol/L.

SGLT2 inhibition lowers reabsorption and increases glucose delivery to downstream SGLT1. Partial recovery by SGLT1 explains why blocking SGLT2 does not cause excretion of all filtered glucose; this does not require assuming compensatory upregulation of SGLT1 expression. The effect depends on glucose concentration, filtration and residual transport. Dapagliflozin and empagliflozin principally inhibit SGLT2; sotagliflozin inhibits both SGLT1 and SGLT2 and should not be treated as pharmacologically identical ([Ghezzi et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30132032/)).

The clinical balance in T1D differs from that in T2D. In the exploratory pooled DEPICT-1/2 analysis, adults with T1D and HbA1c 7.5–10.5% received adjunctive dapagliflozin or placebo for up to 52 weeks. The efficacy population comprised 530, 529 and 532 participants receiving 5 mg, 10 mg and placebo; 55 incorrectly allocated participants were excluded from efficacy analyses but retained in safety analyses. At week 24, adjusted HbA1c changes were −0.40, −0.43 and 0.00 percentage points; at week 52 they were −0.20, −0.23 and +0.07 points. These are within-group changes, not all placebo-adjusted differences ([Phillip et al., 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/169674/1/dom.14248.pdf), Methods §2.5 and Results §3.2).

In the safety populations, adjudicated definite DKA affected 22/548 participants (4.0%) with 5 mg, 20/566 (3.5%) with 10 mg and 6/532 (1.1%) with placebo. Glucosuria can moderate hyperglycaemia while insufficient insulin still permits lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis. Consequently, marked hyperglycaemia is not required for DKA. The trial classified euglycaemic DKA using glucose below 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) near peak β-hydroxybutyrate, not a universal 200 mg/dL cutoff; percentages of “euglycaemic” events depend on that definition. Missed insulin and pump failure were frequently assigned precipitating factors (Phillip et al., Table 2). The physiological implication is that glucose concentration alone cannot establish adequate suppression of ketogenesis.

Renal protection in T1D is not established by these glycaemic trials. In a post-hoc DEPICT analysis restricted to 251 participants with baseline albuminuria, the 52-week placebo-adjusted urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio change was −13.3% (95% CI −37.2 to 19.8) with 5 mg and −31.1% (−49.9 to −5.2) with 10 mg. Corresponding eGFR differences were +3.27 (−0.92 to 7.45) and +2.12 (−2.03 to 6.27) mL/min/1.73 m². Thus, the stronger dose showed an albuminuria signal, but neither eGFR confidence interval excluded no difference. These surrogate findings do not demonstrate slower chronic GFR decline or fewer kidney-failure events in T1D ([Groop et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32946821/), original abstract inspected; full-text appraisal pending). Renal outcome benefits demonstrated in T2D cannot simply be transferred to T1D.
