## 4. The brain's glucose consumption

Cerebral function depends on continuing substrate delivery, transport into tissue and intracellular energy metabolism. Glucose is the principal fuel in the usual fed and overnight-fasted state, but it is not the only fuel the brain can use. The dominant uptake pathways differ from insulin-responsive GLUT4 recruitment in skeletal muscle: GLUT1 and GLUT3 support uptake without requiring an acute rise in insulin. This distinction does not make cerebral metabolism independent of glucose supply, vascular function or insulin signalling ([Mergenthaler et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23968694/)).

For T1D, three consequences follow. Excess exogenous insulin can lower the circulating substrate supply; recurrent hypoglycaemia can diminish warning and counterregulatory responses; and DKA can cause cerebral injury through mechanisms that are not reducible to plasma glucose alone. These processes must not be collapsed into one deterministic concentration-to-injury relationship.

### Transport across the blood–brain barrier and into cells

Blood-to-brain transport crosses both the luminal and abluminal membranes of brain microvascular endothelium. The heavily glycosylated approximately 55-kDa GLUT1 species is prominent in the endothelium; a less glycosylated approximately 45-kDa species is found in glia, and neurons prominently express GLUT3. Glucose phosphorylation and subsequent metabolism maintain an inward concentration gradient, while facilitative carriers permit transport in both directions ([Mergenthaler et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23968694/)).

Membrane distribution is experimentally and species dependent. In rat brain capillaries, Devraj et al. reported a luminal:intracellular:abluminal immunogold labelling ratio of approximately 1:1:2.45; their biochemical membrane work also used bovine preparations. This is not a measured human 2.5-fold transport-capacity ratio. The study further showed that phosphorylation affects antibody recognition, illustrating why measured labelling density must not be equated directly with functional transporter abundance ([Devraj et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21910135/), electron microscopy results and Discussion).

Apparent blood–brain transport parameters likewise depend on what is measured and how compartments are represented. Duarte et al. reanalysed published human and rat brain-glucose data using reversible and four-state conformational models. Their frequently cited Kt range of 1.5–3.5 mmol/L spans **different species and datasets**, not a directly established human normal range. The human four-state fit gave Kt 2.1 ± 5.2 mmol/L (standard error; reported confidence interval 0–12.6), indicating poor precision. A simpler reversible model fitted those data comparably well ([Duarte et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20027232/), Figure 2 and Table 1).

These models explain why bulk brain glucose can be approximately linearly related to plasma glucose over a physiological range despite saturable molecular transport. They do not establish that the barrier is “near saturated” at all euglycaemic concentrations, or that a universal failure point occurs at 3 or 4 mmol/L. Bulk glucose in μmol/g tissue, intracellular concentration, interstitial concentration and glucose-tracer uptake are different measurements. A tissue-to-plasma ratio therefore requires an explicit distribution volume and experimental context before it can be used quantitatively. Molecular transporter affinity is also not a threshold for symptoms or neuronal injury.

### Glucose oxidation, regional demand and glycogen

Mergenthaler et al. summarize an adult cerebral glucose utilization rate of approximately 5.6 mg/100 g/min. For an assumed 1.4-kg brain, that corresponds to a **calculated** 113 g/day, or 31.1 μmol/100 g/min. These are unit conversions from a review-level reference value, not three independently measured constants. By contrast, 25–30 μmol/100 g/min would correspond to approximately 91–109 g/day for the same brain mass. The commonly cited approximately 20% cerebral share concerns whole-body energy demand; it should not be relabelled as a 20–25% share of systemic glucose disposal ([Mergenthaler et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23968694/), introduction and glucose metabolism sections).

Much of cerebral ATP supports ion-gradient restoration, synaptic activity and cellular maintenance. The resting oxygen-to-glucose uptake ratio is close to, but below, the six oxygen molecules required for complete oxidation of one glucose molecule. A reported ratio around 5.5–5.8 is compatible with predominantly oxidative metabolism, while glucose carbon also supports biosynthesis and some net lactate release. It does not establish that precisely 10% of glucose is exported as lactate. Regional rates differ with cell composition, activity and measurement method; a single whole-brain rate should not be assigned unchanged to gray matter, white matter or individual nuclei (Mergenthaler et al.).

Glycogen, located predominantly in astrocytes, provides a small regulated supplement to blood-borne glucose. In a paired study of five healthy adults, Öz et al. used occipital-lobe ¹³C magnetic resonance spectroscopy during two-hour hypoglycaemic and euglycaemic clamps. Glycogen label washout was higher during hypoglycaemia, with glucose averaging approximately 3.2 rather than 5.3 mmol/L. The authors estimated that 0.4–0.6 μmol/g glycogen was mobilized—about 15% of the pool—and that its contribution remained approximately 1% of cerebral glucose utilization under their assumptions. Blood therefore remained the principal glucose source ([Öz et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19502412/), Methods, Results and Discussion).

Their euglycaemic turnover model estimated glycogen content around 3.5 μmol glucosyl units/g tissue. Converting that concentration gives approximately 0.57 g glycogen/kg tissue, or 0.8 g in an assumed 1.4-kg brain; this calculation uses the 162.14 g/mol mass of a glycogen glucosyl residue, not free glucose. Neither this regional, model-derived concentration nor division by a resting metabolic rate establishes a time to loss of consciousness. Pool accessibility, mobilization rate, residual blood supply and changing energy demand matter. A separate five-person experiment found increased glycogen synthesis after hypoglycaemia, but did not establish glycogen supercompensation as the cause of impaired awareness in T1D (Öz et al.).

### Lactate exchange and adaptation to alternative fuel

The astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle hypothesis proposes that astrocytic glutamate uptake increases glycolysis and lactate export, with neuronal uptake supporting oxidation. Monocarboxylate transporters mediate lactate exchange; lactate dehydrogenase converts lactate to pyruvate, which can then enter mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. Lactate oxidation is therefore not completed by lactate dehydrogenase alone. Evidence that the brain can use lactate does not establish the magnitude or obligatory direction of lactate transfer between astrocytes and neurons in vivo. Direct neuronal glucose use, astrocytic oxidation and lactate efflux also have to be included in cerebral carbon and oxygen balances ([Mergenthaler et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23968694/), discussion of neuroenergetic models).

Prolonged fasting provides direct evidence that ketone bodies can replace much of cerebral glucose oxidation, but it is not equivalent to acute insulin-induced hypoglycaemia. Owen et al. studied three adults with obesity after 38–41 days of fasting using cerebral arteriovenous substrate measurements; cerebral blood flow was measured in two. Their calculated oxygen equivalents for β-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate totalled 1.77 mmol/L blood, compared with measured oxygen extraction of 2.96 mmol/L—approximately 60%, calculated from Table V. The authors estimated glucose oxidation at approximately 24 g/day after subtracting lactate and pyruvate efflux and assuming a 1.4-kg brain ([Owen et al., 1967](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/105650/files/pdf), Tables I, IV–V and pp. 1592–1593). The small, selected, sedated sample and prolonged starvation protocol preclude treating these values as acute T1D rescue fractions or a universal 3–7-day adaptation schedule. During hyperinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia, insulin also suppresses peripheral generation of alternative fuels ([McCrimmon and Sherwin, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20876723/)).

### Counterregulation, symptoms and cerebral function in T1D

In healthy adults, controlled lowering of glucose generally suppresses endogenous insulin before recruiting glucagon and sympathoadrenal responses, with prominent symptoms and cognitive impairment developing at lower concentrations. Sprague and Arbeláez summarize counterregulatory recruitment around 3.6–3.9 mmol/L and symptoms around 2.8–3.0 mmol/L; these are review-level approximations from experimental protocols, not a clinical staircase through which every person passes. Prior hypoglycaemia shifts responses downward, whereas chronically elevated glucose can shift them upward. Severe hypoglycaemia is defined by impaired function requiring assistance, not by a universal seizure or coma concentration ([Sprague and Arbeláez, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22783644/), “Glycemic thresholds for hypoglycemic responses” and diabetes sections).

T1D removes the ability to rapidly suppress the delivery of exogenous insulin and often impairs the glucagon response to hypoglycaemia. Failure of changing intra-islet signals is one proposed contributor to the glucagon defect, but pancreatic and central mechanisms remain under investigation; it should not be assigned an invariant five-year onset. Antecedent hypoglycaemia, exercise and sleep can further reduce sympathoadrenal and warning responses, producing **hypoglycaemia-associated autonomic failure (HAAF)**. The effect depends on exposure and context, rather than a single ≥60-minute qualifying event. Impaired awareness and defective counterregulation overlap but are not identical phenotypes, so their historical risk estimates should not be interchanged ([McCrimmon and Sherwin, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20876723/); [Sprague and Arbeláez, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22783644/)).

HAAF is not synonymous with structural autonomic neuropathy. Components of awareness and counterregulation can improve after avoiding recurrent hypoglycaemia; historical intervention studies summarized by Dagogo-Jack reported recovery within two to three weeks in some participants. This demonstrates reversibility of components, not assured or complete recovery in every person ([Dagogo-Jack, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26604275/)).

Evidence for altered cerebral substrate handling must also be separated from evidence for a particular molecular mechanism. Boyle et al. studied 24 insulin-dependent participants stratified by HbA1c and 15 controls. At approximately 3.0 mmol/L, the lowest-HbA1c subgroup did not show a statistically significant fall in cerebral glucose uptake, whereas the other diabetic groups and controls did. This was not a test at 2.5 mmol/L, a randomized comparison of intensive therapy, or a direct measurement of GLUT1 upregulation ([Boyle et al., 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7491135/), original abstract inspected; full-text methods pending). Other experiments have not consistently found increased whole-brain transport after hypoglycaemia, suggesting that exposure, regional responses and alternative substrates may matter ([McCrimmon and Sherwin, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20876723/), pp. 2337–2338). Preserved uptake in one protocol cannot be equated with protection from severe or prolonged hypoglycaemia.

Conversely, symptoms during a fall from chronically high glucose can occur at higher concentrations than in a person accustomed to normoglycaemia. This relative shift in perceived hypoglycaemia does not by itself demonstrate cerebral glucose deprivation at 5–6 mmol/L or prove downregulation of blood–brain barrier GLUT1. Symptom perception, autonomic activation and measured neuroglycopenia are different endpoints.

### Brain injury, cognition and DKA

Long-term cognitive outcomes depend on developmental stage, cumulative glycaemia, vascular complications and exposure definitions; imaging differences cannot automatically be attributed to recurrent hypoglycaemia alone. Earlier DCCT/EDIC follow-up was reassuring about severe hypoglycaemia and adult cognition, but should not be used as an indefinite null result. At 32 years, an observational analysis of 1,051 participants, with median age increasing from 27 to 59 years, associated higher HbA1c, more severe hypoglycaemic episodes and higher systolic pressure with poorer psychomotor and mental efficiency. These associations do not isolate a causal effect of hypoglycaemia, but they contradict an unqualified conclusion that adult cognition is unaffected ([Jacobson et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34051936/), original abstract inspected; full-text appraisal pending). A universal 4–6-point IQ penalty or a precise age-six boundary is not established by the sources appraised here.

DKA-associated cerebral injury is especially important in children. Clinical cerebral oedema is uncommon, whereas imaging or cognitive abnormalities may occur without overt oedema. Azova et al. summarize clinically apparent oedema in approximately 0.3–1% of paediatric DKA episodes and case-fatality estimates of 20–30% among affected cases; these heterogeneous historical estimates are not mortality risks for all children with DKA. Subclinical imaging abnormalities have been observed during treatment and sometimes before it, so “most children already have swelling at presentation” is too specific ([Azova et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33197066/)).

The mechanistic evidence supports overlapping metabolic/cytotoxic injury, altered oxygen delivery or utilization, inflammation, blood–brain barrier permeability and osmotic effects. Hypoperfusion followed by reperfusion is a proposed pathway, not a complete established explanation. Azova et al. specifically caution that human cerebral-flow studies do not demonstrate the profound ischaemia observed in some rodent DKA preparations. Injury or oedema before treatment also shows that treatment-related reperfusion is not necessary in every case. Severe acidosis, hypocapnia and elevated urea identify higher-risk presentations, but correlations with severity or treatments do not prove that any one factor independently causes the injury.

The PECARN trial randomized fluid rate and saline concentration in 1,389 DKA episodes among 1,255 children. Clinically apparent brain injury occurred in 12 episodes (0.9%); the tested regimens did not differ significantly in neurological outcomes ([Kuppermann et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29897851/), original abstract inspected). This challenges a simple claim that faster fluid replacement within those protocols causes cerebral injury. It does not establish equivalence of all possible fluid regimens or generalize securely to profound neurological compromise at presentation; the trial's exclusions and limited number of severe events matter, as discussed by Azova et al. Plasma glucose alone provides neither an oedema threshold nor a sufficient description of the underlying injury.

Sudden unexplained nocturnal death is a separate clinical observation, not a demonstrated cerebral glucose-depletion endpoint. A reported case documented very low sensor glucose near death in a young man with T1D, supporting hypoglycaemia as one possible contributor. A single case cannot estimate incidence, establish fatal arrhythmia, or confirm an orexin-mediated arousal mechanism ([Tanenberg et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19833577/), abstract-level appraisal). These distinctions are important when separating observed outcomes from plausible mechanisms.
